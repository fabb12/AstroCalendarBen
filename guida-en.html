User guide

AstroCalendario di Ben

Everything you need to prepare your first night under the stars. This guide walks you through the app, which is built to answer in an instant the three basic questions every observer asks: what is happening in the sky tonight? Will I be able to see it, or will the mountains hide it? And which way should I look? It all happens right on your device, without depending on the internet or on remote servers: the calculations come to life in real time, while you enjoy the show.

Chapter 00In a nutshell

AstroCalendario is a web application for amateur stargazing. It opens in a browser, can be installed on your phone like a real app, and from then on it works even without a network connection.

No server

The maths happens here

Moon phases, eclipses, rising and setting, conjunctions, Space Station passes: the app computes everything directly on your device, even when there is no network.

No account

Your data stays on the device

Location, journal, telescope profile, the shape of the terrain around your home: everything stays on the device. You can take it elsewhere with a backup file, without creating an account.

Offline

It works in the mountains

The app keeps what it needs to go on working. Without a network you only lose the updated weather, air traffic and new terrain downloads; the sky, on the other hand, stays available.

Two languages

Italian and English

Switching is instant and reloads nothing. Compass points, number formats and countdowns follow the language: in English west is W, not O. This guide exists in both languages too.

Where it works best. On a phone it gives its best, because it uses the compass, gyroscope, GPS and camera: point it at the sky and the map follows your gaze. On a computer it remains excellent for planning, the calendar, the agenda, simulations and the teaching lab, and the planetarium is driven with the mouse.

Chapter 01 · For beginnersThe first ten minutes

This is the only sequence in the manual: six steps, in order, and after the last one the app knows enough about you and your sky to stop giving generic answers. Ten minutes are enough, and you only do it once.

  1. Open the app and install it

    In the header at the top there is Install the app: on a phone it puts the app on the home screen with its icon, and from there it opens full screen without the browser bar. It is not mandatory, but it is what makes it usable in the dark in the garden.

    With Notify me, next to it, you turn on reminders for important events.

  2. Give your location

    It is the piece of data everything else depends on. The app looks for it by itself at start-up with three strategies in cascade, GPS, then network address, then manual choice, and if none of them works it asks you.

    You manage it from Settings → Observation → Manage your position: search for a city by name, accept the GPS, or pick any point on a map (even a mountain pass or a nameless beach, by pasting a Google Maps link).

  3. Say what sky you have

    Settings → Observation → The sky you have: this is the Bortle scale, from 1 (a high-mountain sky) to 9 (a city centre). It is not a cosmetic detail: it decides how many stars are drawn, how visible the Milky Way is, and whether a deep-sky object is suggested as a target or discarded because it would not be visible from there.

  4. Look at the Tonight view

    It is the opening screen and it answers the real question: what do I do tonight? When it gets dark, how much Moon there is, what the sky will be like hour by hour, which objects are worth looking for and when the Space Station passes over. Every row has the Planetarium button, which opens the map already pointed at that object.

  5. Open the Planetarium and point your phone

    From the menu at the bottom. The first time, the phone asks for permission to use its sensors: accept, and from then on the sky follows your movement. If the compass is off (it happens, and it is not the app's fault), there is Calibrate on an object: put the crosshair on the Moon and press the button, and the correction becomes exact by construction.

    Tap an object to open its bubble: four lines attached to the object, with the ⓘ that opens the full card.

  6. Do your first Sky Mission

    In the Tonight view there is the Sky Mission card. Answer five questions (how much time you have, what you observe with, how hard you want it, what you want to look for, which part of the sky you can see) and it prepares two to six stages for you, each introduced by a riddle instead of a name. It is the fastest way to understand what the app can do, and it works wonderfully with children.

    At the end of the evening, mark Seen!: it goes into the Logbook, with its achievements.

A tip on the order. If you are short of time, the steps that really matter are 2 (location) and 3 (your sky). With those two the app is already tuned to you; the others improve precision but can wait for another evening.

Chapter 02The seven views

The bar at the bottom of the screen (at the top on a computer) switches between seven screens. They are not seven menus of the same tool: they are seven different ways of looking at the same evening, from a quick glance to precise pointing.

Tonight

The opening screen · what can be seen tonight from here

Four panels, and everything said only once.

Tonight
When it gets dark (astronomical twilight), how much Moon there is and when it rises or sets.
What sky you will have
All the weather in one place: the verdict for the night, the best window, and the hourly grid in Clear Sky Chart style with seeing and transparency, not just clouds.
What to look at
Planets, the Moon, deep-sky objects and comets in a single ranking, each with the button that opens the planetarium pointed there. Below, the passes of the ISS, Tiangong and Hubble.
Upcoming events
The events on their way. The row opens the planetarium on the evening of the event; the “Card” tab opens the agenda.

The Sky Mission card lives here too (chapter 06).

Month

The grid calendar

Each day carries its events with the category icon and the title. Above the grid there is a single row of controls: the name of the period, two arrows, and the round today button. Tapping the name opens a sheet with the fields.

There, month and date range are two tabs of the same question: the two weeks of holiday, the three months left before the trip, with ready-made durations (7 days, 30, 3 months). There is no limit: ten years of events are computed in batches, with a status line that says how far along it is, and the events appear as they are ready.

Categories: Moon phases, eclipses, seasons, meteor showers, planets, conjunctions, auroras, space stations, and your own personal events.

Agenda

Rich cards for the same events

It follows the month or range chosen in the calendar, but instead of the grid it gives one card per event, answering three questions: can I see it from here? (with the observability traffic light), under what sky? (that evening's weather), with what instrument? (naked eye, binoculars, telescope).

Each card has its buttons: See in the planetarium, Why it happens (which opens the 3D view or the animated lesson), Where to see it (the search for the best spot), Seen! (which sends it to the logbook), plus sharing and export.

Chapter 03The planetarium in depth

The Planetarium is the view that brings almost everything together: point your phone and the sky follows, or drag with your finger. Below, the real landscape of the place where you are. Above, the time machine.

The four panels

Above the map there are four tabs, recognisable by their symbol (clock, calendar, sliders, star). The third is split into five cards, and the card you used last is remembered.

PanelWhat you find there
TimeThe date typed by hand (day, month, year, hours, minutes, seconds), the time step from 10 seconds to 1 year, the run (playback) with its speed, and the place you are looking from.
EventsCalendar events inside the sky: those in progress, those in the coming hours and the next seven days. Tap one and the map points at it; for a meteor shower the radiant appears, for an eclipse the ring around the eclipsed body.
DisplayFive cards: Direction (where I look), Screen (how I look), Objects (what I draw), Sky (the air above me), Landscape (the ground below).
ObjectsThe list of everything you can point at, divided into five families (Sun and Moon, planets, stars, deep sky, stations), with the search by name pinned at the top and the “Up now” filter.

The five cards of “Display”

Card 1

Direction

The four compass points, Follow the phone (the compass drives the view), Centre, Track (the map keeps the object in the middle while the sky moves), Field 55°, and at the bottom the compass with its calibration.

Card 2

Screen

Full screen, clean view (removes everything that sits on top of the sky), night mode (the red filter that does not ruin your dark adaptation), hover, camera, grid and names. The first four close the panel as soon as you tap them, because they change exactly what the panel was covering.

Card 3

Objects

What gets drawn: stars, planets, Sun and Moon, deep sky, constellations and their artwork, comets and asteroids, satellites, aircraft; plus the marks that go with them: the object's track over the surrounding hours, and the ecliptic with its plumb line.

Card 4

Sky

The air above you: the atmosphere (which reddens and dims low objects, as it really does), the forecast clouds, and the aurora, which when switched on also opens its Kp slider.

Card 5

Landscape

The ground below: terrain, relief, town lights, named peaks, water. Each switch has its own status line, which says how far the download has got and, if something is missing, why.

On the map

The column on the right

The controls that must stay available even in full screen, where the panels do not open: camera, full screen, track, record, 3D Solar System, and the two zoom buttons. The compass is at the top right, the time bar at the bottom.

Augmented reality

The camera button (the first one in the column on the map) lays the computed sky over the real image. It is not a rough overlay: a vision engine recognises the Moon, the planets and the silhouettes of aircraft in the frame, matches them with what the computed sky says should be there, and uses that to correct the aim.

What gets corrected is not the position of a label but the frame of reference: that is why an aircraft's label stays on the aircraft even while you turn the phone. A pill at the top left says how many references it is locked on to and with what error; tapping it redoes the aim from scratch, which is useful when you move somewhere else (the metal around you is different).

It works in daylight. In daytime an aircraft is a dark silhouette against the bright sky, not a little light: the detector looks for blobs with their sign, so it finds them anyway.

The time machine

The bar at the bottom of the map is always visible: the round button that brings you back to now, the readout (which opens the Time panel), the two step buttons, the slider and play. With playback the sky moves, one hour per second, one day, one month, and everything moves together: the Moon changes phase, the planets go backwards, the shadow of an eclipse crosses the Earth.

The limits are the year 1600 and the year 3000. It is the same clock used by the 3D view, the eclipse shadow map and the simulations: moving it in one place moves it everywhere.

How far you can zoom

From 180° (the whole sky at once, wide angle) down to a quarter of a degree: at that point the Moon and the planets are true to size, with their faces: the maria, Jupiter's belts, Saturn's rings open as wide as they really are, the polar caps of Mars.

As you zoom in, the sky empties: that is correct, and the app tells you so once. The catalogue goes down to seventh magnitude, and at a quarter of a degree such a small patch of sky does not, on average, contain even one of those stars.

Tapping an object

A tap opens the bubble: attached to the object, with a tail that points at it and follows it while the sky moves. Inside, four lines you can read at a glance.

For a celestial object

What it is, which direction it is in, how high above the horizon, how bright.

For an aircraft

Where it comes from and where it is going, how high and how fast, what aircraft it is.

The ⓘ opens the full card with all the data; the arrow takes you back to the bubble. Tapping a constellation figure opens its page in the atlas instead: who named it, what it is called elsewhere, and whether it can be seen from your home.

The 3D Solar System

The orbit button, on the map, opens the same moment seen from outside. You arrive right on top of the Earth (real coastlines, the boundary between day and night, city lights on the dark side, the dot where you are), passing through an overview frame in which the Earth sits below the Sun and the planets are arranged as you have just seen them in the sky: on the left those of the evening, on the right those of the morning.

From there you zoom out and find everything: each planet with its true face and the phase seen from there, the orbits with their nodes and inclinations, and the two belts of rocks: the asteroids between Mars and Jupiter with the Kirkwood gaps, and the Kuiper belt beyond Neptune, with its cliff at 48 astronomical units.

The interface is three items in a column on the left:

From here
Tonight's arrangement seen from above the Earth.
Everything
The whole Solar System, with the belts.
Earth and Moon
The same view at another scale: the two bodies at their true distance, with the two shadow cones, drawn three times wider than reality so they can be recognised. It is the scene that answers “why doesn't an eclipse happen at every full Moon?”, and you can read it with a ruler.

The eight planets are not everything. There are the minor worlds: Ceres and the three asteroids that in the nineteenth century were considered planets, Pluto, and the icy worlds beyond Neptune that have a name (Eris, Makemake, Haumea, Quaoar, Sedna, Orcus, Gonggong), each with its orbit, almost all tilted and stretched in a way the planets' orbits never are. Together with them the comets visible tonight, the same ones you find in the planetarium's object list.

And the things we built. The two Voyagers, where they are now: at one hundred and seventy and one hundred and forty astronomical units, one thirty-five degrees above the plane of the planets and the other forty-eight below: turn the scene with your finger and you see the V. And, as you approach the Earth, the ISS, Tiangong and Hubble, each with the ring of its orbit: the distance is exaggerated like the Moon's (at four hundred kilometres they would sit inside the Earth's dot), but the direction and the inclination of the plane are real; that is why the ISS passes over Italy and Hubble almost never does.

The search field at the top finds everything in the scene by name (“Saturn”, “Pluto”, “Ceres”, “Voyager 1”, “ISS”), and typing half of it is enough. If what you are looking for belongs to a family that is switched off, it switches it on instead of telling you it is not there.

Behind the ⚙ in the right-hand column: compressed or real distances, enlarged or to-scale sizes, and whether to show or hide the minor worlds and our own spacecraft. Turn it with your finger, pinch to get closer, tap a body to put it in the centre, and from that moment the camera orbits around it.

Recording a moment

The record button, on the map, saves a few seconds of sky in a video (mp4 where available, otherwise webm) to share or keep, with the place and time signature stamped on it. The length is chosen in the Settings: 5, 15 or 20 seconds. Saved videos can be watched again from the Video gallery, in the app header, which also works offline.

Chapter 04The world beneath the sky

This is what sets this app apart from any other planetarium: below the horizon line there is not a black band, there is the place where you are. And it is not there for looks: it tells you whether what you want to see is hidden behind a mountain.

Terrain

The true shape of the horizon

Ground elevations along 120 directions × 18 distances up to 60 km, with the Earth's curvature and refraction (at 60 km they are worth two and a half degrees). From that comes the real ridge in every direction, that is, the answer to “has that object really risen?”.

Relief

Not the outline: the surface

A mesh of 720 directions × 106 rings, with elevations taken from a PNG raster in which every pixel is an elevation, 27 metres apart within 5 km. You see the valley going down, the slope, the groove of the river: things an accumulated ridge cannot tell you.

Colour

Forest, pasture, rock, snow

The bands are not in metres but tied to the snow line of this latitude and this season: in the Alps 2600 m in summer and 1100 m in February. On the plain the line is at three thousand metres and everything stays green, as it should.

Air

Aerial perspective

It is what makes a ridge recognisable as distant. A veil whose opacity is the column of air between you and that piece of mountain: Beer's law written as it is.

Water

Sea, lakes and rivers

The sea with its true perspective (from the shoreline the horizon is 5 km away, and 98% of that distance lies in the first degree below the line), waves as long as tonight's wind makes them, whitecaps above 7 m/s, and the path of light from the Sun or the Moon. Then lakes and rivers, each at its own elevation, with the name written on it as on a map.

Names

Towns and peaks

Towns are named on the horizon, horizontally, in amber, with the distance in kilometres; mountains are attached to their summit, slanted, in the grey-blue of distant rock, with the elevation at the end. Both are veiled by the same air that veils the ridges behind them.

How far to search. In Settings → Planetarium → The horizon around you the three radii (peaks, towns, water) are chosen on a map, not with three numbers in the dark: in the centre is the point the planetarium is looking from, and the three circles are the three radii. Under each name a line tells you how much was found and how much of it the planetarium is drawing right now.

With GPS on, in the car. The landscape does not reload at every traffic light: the validity radius grows with speed (2 km when stationary, 9 in a car, 30 in a plane), the profile is discarded only after a real jump, and the eye height follows the ground under your feet: crossing a pass you really start to see beyond the ridge.

Polar auroras

The auroral oval drawn where it really is, around the geomagnetic pole, squashed towards the night side. Every point is atmosphere between 90 and 420 km (green low down, red high up), and how far away you are decides which of the two peeks over the horizon: that is why the aurora is red from Italy, while the green stays below the horizon.

There is also the Kp slider, to see what storm it would take for something to be visible from here. It is how this view is used almost all the time: a real aurora, from these latitudes, happens once every ten years.

The Milky Way

Not a blurred band: four layers of flakes (the veil, the grainy star clouds, the grains, that is the stars the eye cannot separate, and the objects), drawn from a fractal density field. There is the Great Rift as a ragged spine, the web of dust, the Magellanic Clouds, the knots of glowing hydrogen (Lagoon, North America, Orion), and the colour that shifts from the gold of the bulge to the blue of the outer arms.

It fades with your home Bortle value, and the full Moon almost wipes it out: that is what it really does.

The constellations

All 88, and over the stars you can switch on the drawings of the figures: 29 anchored to the real stars. Tapping one opens the atlas: the story of the figure, the Arabic, Chinese, Māori, Aboriginal and Andean names with what they tell, and the button “take me under its sky”, which moves the planetarium's point of view to the latitude and the night that show it high in the sky.

Chapter 05Things that fly

Real aircraft, space stations, and the moment one of them passes in front of the Sun or the Moon.

ADS-B aircraft

Real air traffic within the chosen radius, drawn in the sky and coloured by distance band:

within 10 km within 20 km within 50 km beyond

The colour is not decoration: they used to be all the same colour, and to know which one was close you had to read the kilometres on every label one by one. Tapping one opens its bubble with the route; the dashed line in front of the nose is where it will be in five minutes, with the minute ticks, from which you can also read its speed without any numbers.

Data and drawing are two switches. The data starts on (when you decide to look, the aircraft are already there) and the drawing off (a starry sky with ten orange labels on top is not what you open at eleven at night). A light next to the button always tells you how the data is doing: fresh arriving old.

ISS, Tiangong and Hubble

The passes of the two space stations and of the Hubble telescope are computed with SGP4 from Celestrak's TLEs, and appear in the Tonight view with time, direction and maximum altitude. The “Go to the planetarium” button opens the map with the clock already set to the moment of the pass, pointed where the station will be, with tracking on: a station crosses the sky in five minutes, and centred only once it would slide out of the frame while you watch it.

At high zoom they are not a diamond: the ISS is 37 pixels wide at a quarter of a degree of field, and you can see the truss with its panels, turned in the direction of travel.

Transits

When an aircraft or a station crosses the disc of the Sun or the Moon, a box placed on the sky tells you who passes in front of what and in how long, with the button that turns the view there.

The calculation that makes everything else necessary: the Sun's disc is half a degree wide, a nearby aircraft crosses the sky at almost three degrees per second, the transit lasts between a tenth of a second and a second and a half. There is no point telling you about it afterwards: you need to get there in time.

Why an aircraft is announced one minute ahead and not ten. A single degree of heading error, over five minutes, moves the aircraft by 1.3 km, which at ten kilometres is more than eight degrees, that is sixteen times the target. Beyond one minute the alert would only say “maybe, somewhere, in that direction”. For a station it is another world: SGP4 with a fresh TLE is off by a tenth of a second, and there the alert gives a time.

Chapter 06Sky Mission

The evening as a treasure hunt instead of a list. It is the feature to try first with people who have never looked at the sky, and with children it is the reason they stay outside.

How it works

Five questions, and from there two to six stages, one at a time. Each stage is introduced by a riddle instead of a name: you are not told what to look for, it is told to you as a story.

The questionWhat it changes
How much time you haveHow many stages: ten minutes give few, two hours quite a lot.
What you observe withEyes, binoculars or telescope. It is a constraint, not a preference: nothing out of reach gets into the evening.
How hardThree levels, which change what you look for and how much you are told before you look.
What you want to look forPlanets, stars, galaxies, constellations, stations and distant worlds. A strict filter: if you ask for galaxies, you hunt galaxies.
Which part of the sky you can seeThe sector that is clear from your balcony or garden.

The three levels

Level 1

Children

Easy targets, and before searching you get the riddle, the sign to look for and the direction. The riddles have a tone of their own, which also covers the hints, the discovery and the final title, not just the frame.

Level 2

Curious

Medium-difficulty targets, and before searching the riddle and the sign. The direction comes with the first hint.

Level 3

Experts

Targets up to the highest difficulty, and only the riddle. Whoever guesses at once has earned a stage with no help.

Distant worlds, which are not searched for in the sky

Pluto, Eris, Sedna, Ceres, the two Voyagers: sixteen targets that cannot be seen in the sky at all (Pluto is fourteenth magnitude, Sedna twenty-first, and a probe is not even an astronomical object). They all sit in their true place in the 3D Solar System, though, and that is where you look for them: when it is the turn of a stage of this kind that view opens full screen, with the riddle on top, and you tap the right little disc instead of pointing your phone. When the stage is over, if the next one is in the sky you go back to the planetarium by yourself: the change of view is not a button to press.

Here the hints speak another geometry, because up there there is no south: they tell you which part of the drawing to be in (the band between Mars and Jupiter, the stretch between the giants, the ring beyond Neptune, the emptiness that comes after) and how far out, in Earth orbits and in light-hours. Zoom the drawing out with − until their band fits in the frame.

Hints, and the solution

If you can't find it, there are three hints, and they are three verses of the same riddle, not a satnav taking over at the second line. Each one still contains the geometry: the direction (including the opposite compass point, “turn your back to the south-east”, which you really use in the dark), the altitude band, and a bright reference to start from with the distance measured in fingers and fists at arm's length, the only protractor everyone carries.

After the third hint the Solution button appears, separate and in another colour. Giving up is not getting it wrong: the stage does not close, and the score takes it into account.

The third hint once revealed the target and centred it, so the hunt ended without anyone having decided so, taking away the only thing a hunt has to give: getting there by yourself.

When you find it

You tap the target on the planetarium map. If it is the right one (the tap tolerance is adjustable in the settings, because a fingertip is not a mouse) the discovery arrives, in four beats:

  1. The cheer, because that is what a person does: first they cheer, then they tell you what you found.
  2. The name, with its kind said with the right word.
  3. A real, imaginable number: in what year that light set off, how many full Moons would fit inside that nebula, how many minutes of light separate us from that planet.
  4. An anecdote: a myth, a discovery or an oddity, and if there is more than one you can ask for another.

And then the question that turns the conversation around: not “here is what it is” but “now you look at it, and tell me what you see”. That last line is what turns a notification into an observation.

Cups and awards

They are two different things. The cup belongs to the evening: gold, silver or bronze depending on how much you found and how many hints it cost you. The awards are forever: they are not won and not lost, they are unlocked and they stay, and they speak of what you saw (five different planets, ten deep-sky objects, three evenings) and not of how good you were. You can look at them in the trophy case.

There is no tin cup: whoever finds nothing has not lost a race, they just had a bad evening, and the voice stays quiet.

The voice. Sky Mission can be listened to, and it has a style for every moment of the hunt: the riddle said softly, the discovery said with excitement, giving up said under the breath, the cup being handed over. With children it goes up a notch. If a speech synthesis service is configured it uses that one, otherwise the device's voice.

Chapter 07The Telescope view

Five panels for those who own an instrument. You declare the profile once (aperture, focal length, eyepieces, Barlow) or choose a preset telescope, and from there everything else is computed.

PanelWhat it does
Instrument Magnification, true field, exit pupil and limiting magnitude for every eyepiece + Barlow combination. And what will I really see: the object's contrast against the sky background of your Bortle value, which is the question that matters and one no catalogue answers.
Alignment The axis to the pole, one step at a time. The phone becomes a spirit level, a protractor and a compass; there is the Polaris clock (where it sits relative to the true pole at this instant) and drift alignment for those who want photographic precision.
Point Three ways. Digital setting circles, star hopping (from the known star to the target, with the chart drawn at the field of your eyepiece), and push-to: rest the phone on the tube and a radar tells you how far to move, in which direction, until you are there.
Session Tonight's plan, ordered the way it is best carried out. And the dew point computed with the Magnus formula: when the optics are about to fog up, and how long is left.
Care Guided collimation and star test, with a drawing of what the diffraction pattern should look like inside and outside focus.

The celestial pole computed in this view is also drawn inside the planetarium, and the local magnetic declination, which matters to an equatorial mount rather than to a planetarium, can be read here.

Chapter 08The laboratory

Eight test benches, one on screen at a time, driven by the real positions of the planets, not hand-drawn animations. Every three-dimensional scene can be turned with your finger and can take over the whole screen with ⛶ (or by turning the phone sideways).

Bench

Why the planets go backwards

Retrograde motion. Above, the plan of the orbits; below, the loop the planet draws in the sky, and the two things moving together.

Bench

Kepler's three laws

The ellipse, equal areas swept in equal times, and the harmony between period and distance, the latter as a graph, because it is a relationship and not a scene.

Bench

The gravitational slingshot

Three cards: the test bench (move the flyby point with your finger and watch how much is gained), the step-by-step maths with the current numbers, and the Voyagers' Grand Tour in 3D, with the two probes really leaving the plane of the planets.

Bench

Launch windows

Why to go to Mars you leave in certain months and not in others, and what happens if you leave outside the window.

Bench

Alignments

When the planets line up, and how well in line they really get. The search looks at every pair, not just the whole group: five planets within a few degrees almost never happens.

Bench

How an aurora lights up

Five frames: the solar wind, the magnetosphere in 3D, the tail that snaps, the oval around the pole, and why it is red from here, the last one drawn to true scale, curvature included, so that the angles can be measured with a protractor on the screen.

Bench

Why constellations do not exist

The stars of a figure placed in real space, each at its measured distance. You fly around the cloud, move away up to two thousand light-years until the figure falls apart, and go and stand on one of its stars (on a planet of Betelgeuse or Rigel), with the sky redrawn from there. In Orion's belt, Alnilam is 1977 light-years away and the other two seven hundred: seen from the side they are no longer in line.

Bench

The setting Sun

Four frames, a single knob (how far the Earth has turned). The Earth turning under your finger, how much air the light crosses (8.5 km of atmosphere against the more than 300 that light travels when the Sun is on the horizon), what colour comes out of the maths, and the same sky on Mars, where everything is reversed: straw-coloured by day and blue around the Sun at sunset, because there it is not molecules that scatter the light but dust.

Each bench ends with the buttons that bring the same thing into the planetarium and into the 3D view. The aurora bench also takes you to another place and another night (Tromsø, Fairbanks, the Po Valley on 10 May 2024) where the aurora really happened.

Chapter 10Automated demos

A demo is a short story the app performs by itself: it moves the place and the clock, points the camera, zooms, runs time forward and flies from the planetarium to the 3D Solar System. It is meant for showing a phenomenon to someone (in class, at the club, at a public evening) without touching anything. At the end everything goes back exactly as it was.

Starting a demo

  1. Open Settings → Automated demos

    The tab shows a list and a card: title, description, duration, number of scenes, date, place, field of view and any simulated Kp of the chosen demo. Below the large Start demo button is the During the demo box: Start in full screen, Record a video of the demo (with the planetarium recorder: the video appears in the planetarium at the end) and Planetarium elements to show, the same switches as the Display tab — stars, names, constellations and their artwork, planets, Sun and Moon, deep sky, Milky Way, grid, ecliptic, atmosphere, clouds, terrain, relief, town lights, mountain names, lakes and rivers and the others. The choices are remembered and apply only to the demo.

  2. Choose one and press “Start demo”

    The settings close (and, if you chose it, the app goes full screen) and the story begins. While the demo is running the app's service notices stay quiet, so they don't cover the phenomenon; a real demo error is always shown. At the bottom the demo bar appears, with the title, the current scene (“scene 1/2 · Planetarium · 8 s”) and the Pause/Resume, Restart and End demo buttons.

  3. Watch, or step in

    You can drag the sky, zoom with two fingers or the mouse wheel, use the arrow keys and change the filters without stopping the demo: as soon as you step in, the camera is yours until the end of the current scene, while the story's clock keeps running. The next scene takes back its own framing.

  4. Leave whenever you like

    Esc or End demo stop it immediately. If you switch to another tab or another app, the demo pauses by itself: when you come back, resume it from the bar.

What is restored. At the end of the demo, or when you stop it, these go back to how they were: the app view, date and time, the place the sky is seen from, the camera direction, the field of view, the planet, Sun and Moon filters, tracking, the time step and playback, the 3D view, the learning-lab bench, the simulated aurora, the planetarium elements chosen for the demo, the recording and full screen. The app's saved position is never touched: the demo's place is only temporary.

Create new

Import demo from file
An .astrodemo or .txt file of up to 100 KB, for example one a colleague sent you. It is checked before opening and overwrites nothing: it becomes a draft.

The editor is in Advanced details. As you type, the line under the text says whether the script is valid or what the problem is, with line and column. While there is an error you can neither save nor start it. The Scene or action template menu with Add scene appends a complete scene at the end, ready to adjust. Keep a copy. Personal demos live only in this browser and are not part of the data backup: if you care about them, export them to a file.

What a script looks like

A script is text, not a program: you describe what should happen and the app performs it. It has three levels: the demo, its scenes one after another, and inside each scene the actions.

define_demo 'moon_from_rome' {
  // A comment is everything after two slashes.
  scene planetarium_view {
    duration: 6s;
    action: set_location { lat: 41.9028, lon: 12.4964, name: 'Rome', timezone: 'Europe/Rome' };
    action: set_date { iso: '2028-12-31T16:30:00Z' };
    action: set_fov { degrees: 6 };
    action: center_target { target: 'Moon' };
  }
  scene planetarium_view {
    duration: 12s;
    action: timelapse { start: 17:30, end: 19:30 };
    action: center_target { target: 'Moon' };
  }
}
  • define_demo gives the name of the demo, in quotes or as a single word without spaces. It is the name you see in the list.
  • scene is followed by the scene type: planetarium_view (the planetarium), transition (the flight to the Solar System), solar_system_3d (the 3D view) or didactic_view (the learning-lab aurora bench, in full screen). The app opens the right view by itself at the start of each scene.
  • duration is required in every scene, in seconds (12s, also 2.5s) or milliseconds (800ms), up to one hour.
  • action is a command with its parameters in braces. Every scene needs at least one. The actions of a scene all start together and last as long as the scene.
  • Every line ends with a semicolon, parameters are separated by commas, text goes in quotes.

The rules that matter

Order within a scene
Actions start together, but they are prepared in the order written. Put set_location and set_date before timelapse: the timelapse times are read in the place and on the day already set.
UTC and local time
set_date is in UTC (the final Z); timelapse uses the place's civil time, daylight saving included. A time that does not exist (02:30 on the night the clocks go forward) is reported before saving.
Scenes chain together
Place, date and field stay as the previous scene left them, until a new scene changes them. Repeat center_target, frame_objects, point_view and highlight_object in every scene where you need them: they only apply to their own.
The camera
The viewer can always take the camera. From that moment the framing actions of the current scene stop; the next scene starts again with its own.
Before starting
A demo does not start with the Solar System open, during a recording or a Sky Mission in progress, or while the planetarium does not yet have a place and catalogues ready: the app says so. The ISS demo also needs the station's orbital data, which the app downloads when you open the demos tab.
Reduced motion
If the system's “reduce motion” preference is on, the decorative rotation of the cameras (3D view and aurora bench) is skipped: the camera is already where the story wants it, while astronomical time keeps running. If the preference changes during a demo, the demo stops and restores.

When something goes wrong

  • “Unknown field”, “Expected ;”, “Unexpected character”: a typing mistake. Look at the line and column shown; often a semicolon or a brace is missing.
  • “Unknown parameter”: the parameter name is wrong, for example fov instead of degrees.
  • “Invalid IANA time zone”: the time zone must be written in full, like Europe/Rome or America/New_York.
  • “This civil time does not exist”: move the timelapse outside the hour skipped by the daylight-saving change.
  • The demo stops halfway with a warning: an action could not continue (for example, no eclipse found). The demo restores everything and the warning says why.

Chapter 13If something doesn't add up

Almost everything that “doesn't work” has an explanation written somewhere in the app. This is the map of where to look.

The symptomWhat to check, and what it usually is
The sky is off by several degrees It is the compass, and almost never the software: nearby metal, a magnetic case, the car dashboard, a reinforced roof shift the field by degrees, and no phone can guess it. Put the crosshair on the Moon or a planet and press Calibrate on an object: the correction becomes exact. The app notices by itself and dims the dial when the compass is disturbed.
The terrain does not appear The pill at the bottom left shows how far the download has got, over four stages. The status line in the Landscape card says the rest: if a source answered “too many requests” the app switches door by itself and retries after 20 s, 90 s, 5 min, 15 min and half an hour. The panel button retries immediately and clears the penalties.
No names on the horizon Mountain names start switched off: turn them on with their button. If they are on and nothing appears, the status line says which case it is: still waiting for the terrain, the service is not answering, there are no named peaks here, or there are but they all stay behind the first ridge.
A nearby lake is not visible The water count says where it lost the stretches: out of range, above eye level, hidden by the terrain. “There is water around here, but the terrain in front hides all of it” is a different answer from “no lake within twenty-five kilometres”.
No aircraft in the sky Look at the light next to the Aircraft button: grey means paused, red a fault (with what it is and when it will retry), green means the data is there, and then check that the drawing is on: data and drawing are two switches.
The stars disappear when zooming in That is correct, and the app tells you once per session: the catalogue goes down to seventh magnitude, and below two degrees of field a patch of sky contains on average half a star. Below 6° the few that remain are drawn one by one, with a halo and a name.
I can't see a change that was just published The installed app may still be showing the previous version. Reload the page or close and reopen the app.
A demo won't start The warning says why. Usually the Solar System is open, a recording or a Sky Mission is in progress, or the planetarium does not yet have its place and catalogues ready: close them, wait a moment and try again. For your own demo, the line under the editor shows the line and column of the error (see chapter 10).
The position is wrong A position chosen by hand is not overridden by GPS or the network, on purpose: to have it recomputed, use Detect again in the position window.

The general rule. In this app an absence is never silent: if something is missing, somewhere it says why. Before blaming the software, look for the status line of the piece that has gone quiet: it is always next to its switch.

Chapter 14Glossary

The words the app uses without explaining them every time.

Altitude and azimuth
The two coordinates of someone looking up: altitude is how many degrees above the horizon (0 = horizon, 90 = zenith), azimuth is the direction counted from north towards east. They are the right coordinates for a pair of eyes; right ascension and declination are the right ones for a mount.
Magnitude
How bright an object is. The lower the number, the brighter it shines: Sirius is −1.5, the naked eye under a good sky reaches 6. Each magnitude is a factor of 2.5 in light.
Bortle scale
How dark your sky is, from 1 (high mountains, the Milky Way casts shadows) to 9 (city centre). It is the setting that changes what the app suggests more than any other.
Seeing
How steady the atmosphere is. A clear but turbulent sky gives dancing stars and a telescope that cannot hold high magnifications. It comes from the jet stream, that is, the wind at altitude.
Transparency
How clean the air is. It is different from seeing and from clouds: a night can be clear, steady and hazy.
Astronomical twilight
When the Sun is 18° below the horizon: from then on it is truly dark, and it is the time from which the app counts the night.
Elongation
How far a planet is from the Sun as seen from here. It decides whether it can be seen in the evening, in the morning, or not at all.
Opposition
The planet on the opposite side from the Sun: it rises at sunset, culminates at midnight, and it is the time of year when it is closest and largest.
Kp index
How disturbed the Earth's magnetic field is, from 0 to 9. To see an aurora from northern Italy you need at least 7, and that is rare.
Deep-sky object
Everything that is neither a star nor a Solar System body: nebulae, clusters, galaxies. The app's catalogue has 142 of them, the full Messier list plus the bright NGCs.
Exit pupil
The beam of light leaving the eyepiece, in millimetres. Wider than the pupil of your eye (6–7 mm when young, less later) means wasted light.
ADS-B
The system aircraft use to broadcast position, altitude and heading. Community networks collect it with volunteers' receivers, which is why coverage has gaps.
Timelapse
Time running faster: minutes or hours of sky in a few seconds. In demos it is written with a start and an end time, in the place's local time.
TLE
The two lines of numbers that describe a satellite's orbit. They age: fresh ones give a pass accurate to a tenth of a second, week-old ones no longer do.

AstroCalendario di Ben, a web application for amateur stargazing. No account, no server, no tracking: the calculations happen on the device and your data stays there. Astronomical information comes from the Hipparcos, Yale BSC, Messier and HYG catalogues; the orbits of planets and minor bodies from the MPC and JPL catalogues distributed by Stellarium.
